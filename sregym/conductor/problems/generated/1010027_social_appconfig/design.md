The incident is a bad rollout of a MongoDB content-budget policy on the existing `post.post` collection. Its limits are ordinary: 4,096 UTF-8 bytes, 2,048 codepoints, and 256 space-separated tokens. A policy compiler has confused scalar text with a sequence of display windows: both boundary ranges advance by one codepoint. It evaluates every nonempty overlapping substring, independently materializing that substring for each budget.

The checks accept the application's normal documents. Their *evaluation* is the problem: quadratic candidate cardinality and repeated UTF-8 traversal/allocation make the work approximately cubic in text length. These checks belong to MongoDB's insert path, outside the application's timing and query-plan logic. Consequently, slow compose requests and database CPU appear while pods, documents, indexes, service discovery, and ordinary Kubernetes configuration remain normal. There is no regex, JavaScript loop, artificial delay, extra service, background writer, or helper resource.

An expert would correlate compose spans with MongoDB insert latency, inspect `currentOp` and slow-operation logs, and then inspect `db.getSiblingDB("post").getCollectionInfos({name:"post"})`. The validator's two `$range` expressions reveal the expanded boundary set. For all three monotone budgets, checking the complete string is sufficient to bound every substring. `mitigate.sh` obtains the actual limits from this live metadata and applies that linear equivalent, retaining independent validator clauses. It is independent of the saved recovery state.

`inject.sh` saves the original collection options/indexes locally once per namespace identity and sets the policy deterministically. It does not change documents, indexes, workloads, observability, or deployment templates. Application restarts and manifest rollbacks do not change MongoDB collection metadata. The policy has no expiry or automatic recovery.

`recover.sh` restores the saved options. If the original collection had no explicit validation metadata, MongoDB cannot remove those option fields through `collMod`; recovery briefly quiesces the frontend and post writer, makes a lossless copy with the original options/indexes, and atomically renames it into place. All committed documents and 64-bit values are retained, temporary collections disappear, and replica counts return to their prior values. Collection identity changes only when such a rebuild is necessary.

Initial live evaluation showed sustained throughput around 35–57 requests/second against a 100 requests/second target, p99 measurements of several seconds against a 1-second limit, and MongoDB CPU around 1.5 cores versus approximately 0.023 cores before the session. Formal script validation is recorded separately.

Formal validation completed the following sequence: healthy baseline; two consecutive injection runs; two violated 120-second windows separated by more than 60 seconds (11/11 rounds each); replacement of the post-storage RPC pod followed by another 11/11 violated window; two consecutive mitigation runs followed by 11/11 healthy rounds; two consecutive recovery runs followed by exact collection-option/index comparison and 11/11 healthy rounds; reinjection. The repeated cleanup retained 13,970 and then 13,980 committed documents. Final submission measurements are in the accompanying JSON snapshots.

Final reinjection checks were separated by more than 60 seconds and both reported 11/11 violating rounds. The first measured 42.6–66.8 requests/second and p99 6,920 ms; MongoDB CPU was 2.237 cores against a 0.023-core baseline. The second again reported 11/11 violating rounds and a 1.653-core MongoDB CPU surge. The incident is left active for submission.
